# Phishing: verify the request, not just its appearance

Phishing messages try to persuade someone to reveal information or take an unsafe action while appearing trustworthy. A familiar logo or display name does not establish who sent a message.

Look at the request: Does it unexpectedly ask for credentials, financial information, an attachment to be opened, or immediate action? Urgency is a warning sign to investigate, not proof by itself. Correct grammar does not establish legitimacy.

Avoid using the suspicious message's links or phone number to verify itself. Use a known bookmark, official application, or independently obtained contact information. Follow your organization's reporting process.

Do not reply with a password or send a credential as proof that you own an account. If you already entered credentials or approved an unexpected request, report what happened promptly through a trusted channel and follow the service's recovery process.

## A fictional example

A message says the shared study account will close in ten minutes unless you confirm a password. The display name says "Study Support," but that alone is not identity verification. Open the service through a known route and check with the responsible team.

The goal is to verify whether the request is legitimate before disclosing information, not to decide from spelling alone.
